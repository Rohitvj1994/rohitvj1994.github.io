How to read the air calculator.

The calculator takes one number, an AQI, and answers three plain questions: what is in the air, how much of it do I breathe in, and what should I do? This guide explains every tab and every number, the maths behind them, what was assumed and where the figures come from.

01 · The idea in one minute

An AQI is a grade, not a weight.

AQI 350 tells you the air is in the "Very Poor" band. It does not tell you how much pollution you actually take into your body. That depends on three things: how dirty the air is, how hard you are breathing and for how long.

Inhaled PM2.5 = Concentration × Breathing rate × Time (µg) (µg per m³) (m³ per hour) (hours)

One worked example runs through this guide. Enter AQI 350 and the calculator says:

StepResultIn plain words
AQI → PM2.5185 µg/m³Each cubic metre of air holds about 185 micrograms of fine particles, if PM2.5 is what set the AQI.
Sitting for 8 hours373 µgA resting adult breathes about 2 m³ of air in that time.
Running for 1 hour555 µgOne hour of running takes in more than eight hours of sitting.
Against WHO12.3×About twelve times the WHO 24-hour reference level of 15 µg/m³.

03 · Tab by tab

What each tab shows you.

All ten tabs share the same inputs. Change the AQI, your age group or your activity in one place and every tab updates.

Tab 1Home

The answer at a glance

You enter
An AQI between 0 and 500.
You see
The band, the PM2.5 estimate in µg and mg, the WHO multiple, the inhaled mass for your chosen activity, and what to do at this level.
How to read it
The dial shows where your number sits on the six bands. Copy report gives a text summary you can paste into a message.
Tab 2AQI Converter

From an index back to a concentration

You enter
An overall AQI. Or, lower down, your own readings for up to eight pollutants.
You see
The PM2.5 estimate, the formula with your numbers filled in, and the full CPCB breakpoint table.
How to read it
The PM2.5 figure is conditional. It is right if PM2.5 is the pollutant that set the AQI. If PM10 or a gas set it, real PM2.5 is lower. So treat it as an upper estimate.
Tab 3PM2.5 Calculator

Use a real reading if you have one

You enter
A measured PM2.5 value in µg/m³, from a monitor or an official station.
You see
Its CPCB sub-index and band, the same value in mg/m³, and a one-cubic-metre box of air with that load in it. You can also compare two places.
How to read it
Once entered, the whole calculator uses your measured value in place of the AQI estimate. 1 mg = 1,000 µg, so 185 µg/m³ is 0.185 mg/m³.
Tab 4Human Exposure

How much you breathe in

You enter
Age group, activity and duration. Advanced users can type their own breathing rate.
You see
Air volume breathed, PM2.5 inhaled in µg and mg, the calculation line by line, and a "what if" table.
How to read it
This is the mass entering the nose and mouth. Part of it is breathed out again. It is useful for comparing choices, such as running outdoors against resting indoors.
Tab 5Activity Simulator

Same air, different effort

You enter
Age group and duration.
You see
A bar chart and table of inhaled mass for nine activities, from sleeping to heavy labour.
How to read it
For an adult, running moves about twelve times more air than sitting. Effort matters as much as the AQI.
Tab 6Indoor vs Outdoor

Your whole day, block by block

You enter
Blocks of your day: place (indoors or outdoors), effort and hours. Indoor PM2.5 if you have measured it, or an assumed indoor/outdoor ratio.
You see
The day's total, the split between indoor and outdoor hours, and a line that climbs through the day.
How to read it
The example day at AQI 350 totals about 1,321 µg: 852 from 21 indoor hours and 468 from 3 outdoor hours. Three hours outside give over a third of the day's load.
Tab 7WHO Comparison

A reference line, not a verdict

You enter
Nothing new.
You see
Your PM2.5 beside the WHO 24-hour level (15 µg/m³) and, separately, the WHO annual level (5 µg/m³), with India's standards for context.
How to read it
The 24-hour level applies to a full-day average. The annual level applies only if air like this lasted a year. WHO found no level below which PM2.5 is harmless, so lower is always better.
Tab 8Health by Age

Precautions for you

General advice for the band, a note for your age group, and extra precautions for each condition you selected. Below that, how pollution affects lungs, heart, brain, children and pregnancy.
How to read it
This is education, not diagnosis. There are no risk percentages here, on purpose.

Masks and purifiers, as "what if"

With an assumed 70% respirator reduction outdoors, the example day falls from 1,321 to about 993 µg. The day falls by less than 70% because the mask covers only three hours.

04 · The maths in one place

Five formulas, nothing hidden.

1. AQI to PM2.5

CPCB draws a straight line between breakpoints. The calculator runs that line backwards.

C = B_low + (AQI − I_low) ÷ (I_high − I_low) × (B_high − B_low) AQI 350 sits in the 301–400 band, where PM2.5 runs from 120 to 250: C = 120 + (350 − 300) ÷ 100 × 130 C = 185 µg/m³

2. Micrograms to milligrams

mg = µg ÷ 1,000 185 µg/m³ = 0.185 mg/m³

3. Inhaled mass

D = C × IR × T Adult sitting for 8 hours at 185 µg/m³: D = 185 × 0.252 × 8 D = 373 µg = 0.373 mg

4. A whole day

Total = D₁ + D₂ + D₃ + … Each block of the day has its own C, IR and T. Indoor C = Outdoor C × indoor/outdoor ratio 185 × 0.6 = 111 µg/m³

5. Protection and the WHO multiple

Adjusted C = C × (1 − assumed reduction) With an assumed 70% reduction while the mask is on: 185 × (1 − 0.70) = 55.5 µg/m³
WHO multiple = C ÷ guideline 185 ÷ 15 = 12.3

05 · Assumptions

What the calculator takes for granted.

  • PM2.5 set the AQI. When you enter only an AQI, the PM2.5 figure assumes PM2.5 was the worst pollutant. Otherwise the true value is lower.
  • The AQI is a 24-hour figure. CPCB's index uses 24-hour averages for particles. A reading at one moment can be higher or lower.
  • Above AQI 400, the line continues. CPCB gives no upper PM2.5 value for "Severe". The calculator extends the line to 380 µg/m³ at AQI 500 and marks these values as indicative.
  • Breathing rates are population averages from a US dataset, matched to the nearest age band. Your own rate depends on body size, fitness and effort.
  • Activities share five effort levels. Brisk walking and cycling, for example, use the same rate.
  • Indoor air is a ratio of outdoor air unless you enter a measurement. The default is 0.6 with windows closed and 0.9 with them open. Cooking, incense or smoking can push indoor air above outdoor air.
  • Mask and purifier reductions are yours to set. The starting values (70% and 50%) are illustrations, not measured performance.
  • The mask is worn only outdoors and the purifier runs only indoors.
06 · Precautions built in

What the calculator deliberately does not do.

It will not invent harm numbers

  • No percentage of lung damage.
  • No years of life lost.
  • No probability of disease or death.
  • No age-based "damage multiplier".

It labels what is uncertain

  • Estimates from AQI are marked "conditional".
  • Ratios and reductions are marked "assumption".
  • WHO levels are shown as a reference, with their averaging period.
  • Health text describes population research, not your diagnosis.

Why no harm score? Inhaled mass can be calculated. The share that stays in the lungs depends on particle size and how each person breathes, so it is left out. Disease risk comes from long-term studies of large populations and cannot be worked out for one person from one AQI reading.

07 · Limits

Where to be careful.

  • This is an educational tool. It does not replace advice from your doctor.
  • PM2.5 is the only pollutant carried through the exposure tabs. Ozone, nitrogen dioxide and others matter too.
  • Adding up micrograms over days is a fair way to compare routines. It is not a measure of damage stored in the body.
  • The calculator does not fetch live data. You bring the AQI or the PM2.5 reading.
  • Use the results to compare options and to decide when to cut back outdoor effort, not as precise personal doses.

Glossary

AQIAir Quality Index. A 0–500 grade built from the worst pollutant of the day.
CPCBCentral Pollution Control Board, the agency that defines India's AQI.
PM2.5Particles 2.5 micrometres across or smaller, about 30 times thinner than a hair. Small enough to reach the deep lung.
PM10Particles up to 10 micrometres. Includes PM2.5.
µg/m³Micrograms per cubic metre of air. A microgram is one millionth of a gram.
Sub-indexThe 0–500 score for one pollutant. The highest sub-index becomes the AQI.
Breathing rateVolume of air breathed per hour, in cubic metres. Also called inhalation rate.
24-hour averageThe mean concentration over a full day. Both the AQI and the WHO short-term level use it.
N95A respirator that filters at least 95% of test particles in a laboratory. Real protection depends on fit.
